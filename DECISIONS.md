# Decision log

Short, dated notes on decisions that affected the build. Newest at the bottom. Assumptions made where the brief was silent are marked **Assumption**.

## 2026-11-11

- **Track 1, policy administration.** Fullstack TypeScript: Next.js (App Router), Prisma, Postgres. One codebase keeps the frontend and backend types in sync, and Postgres gives us real transactions for financial writes.
- **One product with flat per-coverage pricing.** A Small Business Package with General Liability ($1,095/yr, required), Cyber ($730/yr), and Business Property ($365/yr). The prices are chosen to work out to whole dollars per day so the math is easy to verify live. **Assumption:** no rating factors, taxes, or fees.
- **Money in integer cents, rounded per line.** Each pro-rated line is rounded half-up to the cent, and totals are sums of rounded lines. This avoids floating-point drift and makes every statement line reproducible by hand.
- **Daily pro-rata with actual days in term.** `annual × days covered ÷ days in term`, start date inclusive, end date exclusive. **Assumption:** the brief doesn't specify a convention, and this is the common industry one.
- **Append-only ledger, and corrections reverse and rebook.** No ledger row is ever edited or deleted. A correction posts a reversal of the original charge and a new charge, so the brief's requirement to keep "the original charge and the correction visible" falls out of the design rather than being a special case.
- **Cancellation is pro-rata with no penalty.** Refund equals paid minus earned premium, sent back through Stripe Refunds. **Assumption:** no short-rate penalty or minimum earned premium.
- **Payment status comes only from Stripe webhooks.** The browser redirect is never trusted to mark a payment as paid. This is what makes "pending, succeeded, failed" honest.
- **Integrations: Stripe test mode (live) and Resend email (live, sandbox).** *(Superseded below by DocuSign.)* Carrier, underwriting, and policy documents are simulated. Every integration call and webhook is stored in an integration log for evidence.
- **Left out: installments.** Pay-in-full only. This keeps "amount due" a single number per policy, and installments wouldn't exercise anything new in the pro-rata logic.
- **Credits are applied first, then refunded automatically.** When a correction or other change leaves the customer with a credit (a "return premium"), the credit first pays down any open balance on the policy. Anything left is refunded to the original card through Stripe without the broker having to act. This matches how carrier billing systems handle return premium, and it avoids leaving the customer's money sitting idle. **Assumption:** we have no minimum refund threshold, although real systems often hold very small credits.
- **Hosting and services on free tiers only.** Vercel Hobby, Neon free Postgres, Stripe test mode, and DocuSign developer sandbox. None of them needs a credit card. 
- **Second integration is DocuSign, not Resend.** The policyholder e-signs the application (embedded signing in our app) before the broker can bind the policy, and a DocuSign Connect webhook records completion. We chose it over email because it belongs to the insurance workflow (signed application before binding) rather than being generic plumbing. The cost is about 2 to 3 hours of setup (JWT auth, document tabs, webhook verification). Email notifications are left out and can be added later.
- **Brokers edit policies only through dated changes (endorsements).** Brokers can edit coverage, correct any past change (including the original issue), and edit non-money details. Every edit is a new history entry, and money-affecting edits post ledger lines rather than overwriting anything, so each amount stays traceable. Manual premium overrides and term extensions are left out because they add rules without exercising new money logic.
- **One "Edit coverage" action instead of separate add and remove.** The broker picks an effective date and turns coverages on or off in a single change. One change can post several ledger lines (a charge per coverage added, a credit per coverage removed), all tied to the same history entry. This matches how a broker thinks about it ("change the coverage from this date") and keeps the history to one entry per decision.
- **Next.js Cache Components turned off.** The Next.js 16 starter enables Cache Components, which prerenders and caches parts of pages. Every page in this app is per-user financial data that must be fresh on each request, so caching adds risk and no benefit. All pages render on demand.
- **Prisma pinned to 7.10.1.** npm's `latest` tag for the Prisma CLI currently points at an 8.0 release candidate. We pinned the CLI to the stable 7.x line that matches `@prisma/client`.
- **Demo auth is a signed cookie with seeded users.** Sessions are an HS256-signed, httpOnly cookie, and passwords are bcrypt hashes. There's no sign-up, password reset, or third-party identity provider, because reviewers only need two demo logins. **Assumption:** each policyholder login belongs to exactly one customer.
- **Plain Tailwind instead of a component library.** The plan mentioned shadcn/ui. We're using hand-written Tailwind components instead, to keep the dependency list small.
- **Premium is always calculated by replaying the policy's history.** One pure function (`src/lib/engine/replay.ts`) applies the issue, coverage changes, and cancellation in order and returns every premium line. A correction replays the history with the corrected action in place of the original, compares the result with what's on the statement, and posts only the reversals and new lines needed to close the gap. This means a correction can never disagree with the original calculation, a correction that would break a later change is refused with a clear message, and the same action can be corrected more than once.
- **Fixed 12-month term.** The broker chooses the start date and the end date is one year later. "Days in term" is therefore 365, or 366 if the term includes Feb 29, and a full term always costs exactly the annual premium. **Assumption:** short-term policies aren't needed for the demo.
- **Refunds go back against the newest card payment first.** A card refund has to be tied to a specific payment and can't exceed what's left of it, so a large refund can be split across several payments, newest to oldest.
- **Every new customer gets a policyholder login automatically.** Creating a customer also creates a login with the customer's email and the shared demo password, so a reviewer can create a customer as the broker and immediately sign in as that policyholder. A real platform would send an invitation instead.
- **Corrections can change the policy start date.** Correcting the original issue to a different start date shifts the whole 12-month term, so every premium line is reversed and rebooked against the new term. The same replay-and-compare code handles it with no special case.
- **Payment status is decided by Stripe's servers, through two doors.** The webhook is the main one. As a backstop, opening a policy page asks Stripe directly about any payment or refund that is still unsettled, so a missed or delayed webhook can't leave a payment stuck. Both paths run the same idempotent code, so hearing about a payment twice posts one statement line. The browser is never trusted to say a payment succeeded. This refines the earlier "webhooks only" decision.
- **Four payment states.** "Not submitted" (the payment form was opened), pending (Stripe is processing), succeeded, and failed. A failed attempt keeps its own record, and its Stripe PaymentIntent is cancelled before a new attempt starts, so an old attempt can never complete on top of a new one.
- **A payment always covers the full amount due.** No partial payments, in keeping with pay-in-full. If the amount due changes while a payment form is open, the unfinished PaymentIntent is updated to the new amount.
- **Refunds reach the statement only when Stripe confirms them.** Until then the statement shows a credit and the payments table shows the refund as pending. If Stripe rejects a refund, the credit stays visible and the broker gets a "Refund credit" button to retry.
- **Card and US bank account only.** Stripe's form would otherwise also offer Cash App, Amazon Pay, and Klarna, which don't fit paying a commercial insurance premium. Cards settle immediately, and bank debits give a genuine pending state before they clear.
- **Customer details are edited in place, with a history entry on every policy.** Name, contact, and address don't affect any amount, so the customer record is updated directly. Each change is written to the history of every policy the customer holds (field, old value, new value, who, when). Changing the contact email doesn't change the policyholder's login.
- **Corrections explain themselves in plain English.** Each correction's history entry says how many days changed, the daily rate, the old and new premium, and the net result, built from the same statement lines the customer sees. The debrief asks what the customer sees, and this is the answer.
- **A signed application comes before the policy.** With DocuSign configured, the broker sends an application, the policyholder signs it inside the app (DocuSign embedded signing), and only then can the broker issue the policy. The signed PDF, with DocuSign's certificate of completion, is linked from the policy and the customer page. The application isn't part of the ledger: no money is owed until the policy is issued. If DocuSign isn't configured (local development without keys), the broker issues directly.
- **"Signed" is decided by DocuSign's servers.** Like payments, there are two triggers, a per-envelope webhook and the policyholder's return from the signing screen, and both only cause a fresh status check against DocuSign's API. The webhook body is never trusted, so it needs no shared secret: a forged call can do nothing but trigger a status check.
- **DocuSign through its REST API directly, not the SDK.** Four calls (create envelope, signing link, status, download) plus the JWT sign-in are about 200 lines with no extra dependency. The code refuses to run against anything but DocuSign's sandbox hosts, as the Stripe code refuses live keys.

## 2026-11-11 (extensions)

The brief names claims, renewals, and broker commissions as extensions once the core workflow works. Each is built in its simplest honest form.

- **Commission is derived, not stored.** The broker earns a flat 10% of premium. Commission is calculated from the premium lines on each policy's statement, so every dollar of commission points at the statement line behind it and the two can't disagree. A credit or reversal earns negative commission, which is the clawback when premium is returned. Rounding is to the cent with halves away from zero, so reversing a line always reverses its commission exactly. The rate is saved on the policy at issue so a later rate change wouldn't rewrite the past. **Assumptions:** commission is earned when premium is charged, not when it's collected, and paying the broker isn't modelled.
- **A renewal is a new policy, linked to the old one.** It covers the 12 months after the current term, carries forward the coverages in force on the last day of that term (a coverage removed mid-term isn't renewed), and is priced from today's rate table. Giving it its own number, statement, and payments keeps each term's money traceable separately. A policy can be renewed once, and a cancelled policy can't be renewed. **Assumptions:** a renewal is on terms the customer already signed, so it needs no new application, and later changes to the expiring policy don't flow into a renewal that already exists.
- **Claims are kept off the premium statement.** Claim money flows from the insurer to the customer, while the statement tracks what the customer owes in premium. Mixing them would make "amount due" wrong. Each claim has its own traceable record: who filed it, who decided it, when it was paid, and how the payout was calculated.
- **A claim is covered only if its coverage was in force on the date of loss,** judged by the policy's history as it stands now. This ties claims to the same effective dates the reviewers' scenario corrects: moving a coverage's start date earlier can bring a loss into cover, and a claim whose coverage is later corrected or cancelled away from its loss date is flagged and can't be approved.
- **Payout = approved loss − $500 deductible, capped at the coverage's per-claim limit.** The deductible and limit are saved on the claim when it's approved. **Assumptions:** one flat deductible, fixed per-claim limits with no annual aggregate, and no partial payments or reserves.
- **Claim payments are simulated.** Marking a claim paid records the payment in the app. No money moves, because paying a customer would need a payout integration that the brief doesn't ask for.
- **Integration tests run against a separate local test database.** They exercise the real service code, including the reviewers' scenario, without Stripe or DocuSign. The test setup refuses to run against any database that isn't local.

## 2026-11-12

- **Premium is due on the term's start date.** A policy whose term hasn't started shows "due by" its start date in place of a bare "amount due", and can be paid early. This matters for renewals, which the broker can create well ahead of the new term. **Simplifications:** a real system would only offer renewal 60 to 90 days before expiry, send a renewal notice, and cancel an unpaid renewal back to its start date after a grace period. We let the broker renew at any time so the flow is easy to try, and leave out notices, grace periods, and lapse for non-payment.
- **A credit is also refunded when a payment succeeds, not only after a broker's change.** The amount due can drop while a payment is still clearing (a bank debit takes days). When that payment lands for the old, higher amount, the extra is refunded straight away, by the same refund code a correction uses. Before this, the credit waited for the broker to press "Retry refund", which contradicted "refunded automatically" above.
- **An open payment form follows the amount due down, never up.** If a broker's change lowers the amount due while the customer has the payment form open, the unfinished Stripe payment is lowered to match, or cancelled if nothing is due. If the change raises the amount due, the open payment is left alone: the customer agreed to the amount on their screen, so they pay that and the rest stays due. This is the one case where a payment covers less than the full amount due.
- **Policy numbers come from a database sequence.** They used to be "number of policies, plus one", which gives two policies issued at the same moment the same number. A Postgres sequence can't repeat. **Trade-off:** a number is used up even if issuing fails, so numbers are unique but can have gaps.
- **Payment tests use a stand-in for Stripe.** The refund and open-payment rules are tested against the real database with a fake Stripe client that records what it was asked to do, so the tests make no network calls.
- **A policy and its renewal never overlap, and a gap between them is only allowed on purpose.** An overlap would charge the customer twice for the same days and put a claim under two policies, so it is always refused. A gap (days with no cover) does happen legitimately, for example after a lapse, so the broker can choose one, but only with a written reason. The gap is then shown on both policies and noted in both histories.
- **A renewal follows the policy it renews by default.** When a correction moves the end of a policy's cover, its renewal is moved to start that day, and the renewal's own renewal follows in turn. When a renewed policy is cancelled, the renewal is cancelled from its first day, which credits everything charged on it. Both policies are saved together or not at all, and each keeps its own history entry and statement lines. The broker can instead leave the renewal where it is, if that leaves a gap rather than an overlap. If the renewal can neither follow (for example, one of its own changes would fall outside its new term) nor stay, the change is refused. This replaces the earlier assumption that changes to an expiring policy never reach its renewal. Coverage changes still don't.
- **A renewal's own start date can't move into the term it renews.** Moving it later is a gap and needs a reason. To move both policies, the broker corrects the earlier one.
- **History notes.** A new history entry type, "Note", records a fact that changes no amount. It is used for coverage gaps.
- **A read-only window onto the deployed database.** `npm run prod:query` lets us confirm what happened in production (a signature, a payment, a refund) without relying on screenshots. The connection string lives in a git-ignored local file and is never printed. The script can't change data: the session is set read-only, each query runs in a read-only transaction that is rolled back, and queries are sent as prepared statements so only one statement is accepted.
